# WHO MAY CHANGE WHAT
## Governance permissions as morphological operators for multi-stakeholder renewal in Shanghai's Dapuqiao district

REMOVED FOR PEER REVIEW

**Abstract.** This paper is not a design tool, and it is deliberately not a parametric toy. It is a measurement instrument, and what it measures is the price of a distribution of authority. Renewal in historic districts is decided less by what a design optimises than by who is permitted to alter which buildings. Generative tools expose form or performance parameters to a single user; participatory tools collect preferences over predetermined options; neither represents how transformation authority over an existing building stock is distributed. We encode that distribution directly and then measure what it costs. In Shanghai's Dapuqiao (Tianzifang) district, 1,370 buildings are attributed to four classes — 780 resident-held, 344 developer/operator-held, 183 public assets, 63 unclassified — and three governance scenarios are written as permission tables binding six morphological operators to particular classes under one shared regulatory envelope. Five groups are compared under identical inputs, objectives and constraints: the district with nothing changed; public coordination, development growth and resident-and-heritage priority; and, as a fifth group, a parametric optimum searched without reading any label. Because permissions are attributed, the addable envelope contracts before any search: densification alone could add 14.8–17.7% of the site area if every class were permitted, but only 0.8–2.5% once it is confined to the class each table allows, and not at all under resident-and-heritage priority. The releasable envelope is similarly small, 0.3–0.5% of the 5 m street buffer, and buying it costs 10–19% of the district's floor area. Against that envelope, each group is searched with NSGA-II on three shared objectives (residential exposure, net floor-area change, street-adjacent ground release), with gross additions and gross removals reported beside the net, and three role perspectives select from the resulting Pareto sets a posteriori. ⟨E0/E1: one-sentence headline result⟩ The contribution is an auditable two-stage mapping — who may change what, then how to choose — that separates what the instrument can compute from what a single run shows, and makes the price of each governance setting legible at building resolution.

**Keywords.** Urban renewal; stakeholder governance; multi-objective optimisation; a posteriori preferences; heritage districts; research instrument.

---

## 1. Introduction

Shanghai has turned its renewal policy from demolition-led redevelopment to retention: the principle of "retain, renovate, demolish" was codified in the Shanghai Urban Renewal Regulations (Standing Committee of the Shanghai Municipal People's Congress, 2021), and the master plan caps total construction land, so new capacity must come from the existing stock. Under these conditions the decisive question in a historic district is no longer what to build on cleared land but who may transform which existing building, by how much, and at whose expense. Tianzifang, the lilong-and-factory core of the Dapuqiao subdistrict, makes the question concrete. Its spatial evolution has been read as the product of power relations among the district government, operators and residents (Wang & Zhou, 2022); its original residents have become landlords who capture rent from heritage (Arkaraprasertkul, 2018); and the district's management rules require at once the protection of characteristic buildings, the improvement of community facilities and the coordination of commercial and residential uses (Huangpu District People's Government, 2023).

Computational urban design has tools for conflicting objectives but not for conflicting claimants. Evolutionary multi-objective methods return a Pareto set of alternatives (Koenig et al., 2020), yet the objectives belong to no one, and the field's own systematic review names limited stakeholder integration as a standing gap (Jiang et al., 2024). Participatory tools bring many people in, but mostly as assessors of predetermined options, rarely beyond consultation (Hanan & Carhart, 2025; Goncalves et al., 2026). A tool that lets a user adjust parameters and compare scenarios does not by itself represent stakeholders, however its sliders are labelled.

**What this paper is and is not.** It does not propose a deeper design tool, and it is not a parametric toy to be played with until an attractive form appears. It proposes a narrow instrument: a way of writing down who may change what, and a way of measuring what that distribution costs. Its output is not a preferred scheme but a set of commensurable quantities — how much of the attainable envelope a permission table concedes, where the table reaches configurations that unattributed parameters cannot, and how much of either survives changes in labels and measures. The instrument is therefore judged by whether those quantities are computed correctly and reported honestly, not by whether its forms are attractive.

We argue that stakeholders differ from users not in what they prefer but in what they can claim. Classes of actors hold, or are taken to hold, different parts of the same stock, and a permission granted to one class is exercised on buildings whose effects fall on others: a developer's densification raises exposure for residents in the same block. We therefore represent governance before generation, as permission over morphological operators (which classes' buildings may be altered, by which operators, at what intensity), and preferences after generation, as role-specific orderings over a shared Pareto set (Kaim et al., 2020). The model represents the authority to transform buildings under given regulations, not the authority to set those regulations; the latter enters as fixed, editable premises (Section 4.2). Earlier work by the authors read stakeholder power through a height-only transform across several districts (REMOVED FOR PEER REVIEW); this paper couples permissions to operator-level form generation and to multi-objective search on one heritage site.

**Research question and design.** The question is whether assigning transformation authority to stakeholder classes produces trade-offs that a parameter tool working under the same premises cannot reach, and that the same quantity of permission, assigned without regard to who holds the buildings, does not reproduce. Answering it requires a comparison rather than a demonstration. Five groups are searched under identical data, objectives, constraints and budget: the existing district with nothing changed; public coordination, development growth and resident-and-heritage priority; and a fifth group in which the generator reads no label at all (Sections 4.1 and 4.6). Before any of them is searched, the envelope each table makes available is computed analytically, so that "what the table allows" and "what the search found" are never conflated.

The contributions are three. (1) An auditable two-stage mapping from governance permissions to form, and from role priorities to selection, recorded building by building. (2) A measurement design that separates what the instrument can compute from what this run shows: a five-group comparison, a permission ceiling derived from each table before search, a permission-matched null model, and robustness tests on labels, exposure scale and tolerances. (3) A reading of the Dapuqiao results in terms of who bears which part of each trade-off — including the finding that, in this district, attribution removes an order of magnitude of the addable envelope before the optimiser is switched on.

## 2. Related work

**Objectives without owners.** Procedural and evolutionary generators turn designer-set targets into form and resolve contradictory goals as non-dominated sets (Koenig et al., 2020; Miao et al., 2020). Metric-to-form mappings make morphology indicators the entire specification of a block (Cai et al., 2025), and interactive generators let a designer explore alternatives from a handful of parameters (Talmor Blaistain & Fisher-Gewirtzman, 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). In all of these the quantities that renewal politics fights over — intensity, coverage, height — are set, but not attributed to anyone.

**Preferences without claims.** Participatory systems collect votes, comments or mapped preferences; the closest CAADRIA precedent lets citizens propose and vote on heritage interventions while noting that citizens' power over the decision remains limited (Leong & Janssen, 2022). Reviews against Arnstein's (1969) ladder find most digital tools at consultation (Hanan & Carhart, 2025). Where values are elicited from citizens, they are often found to lie outside the design space being explored (Knecht et al., 2019); simulated resident agents optimise satisfaction rather than represent parties to a decision (Zhou et al., 2024).

**Claims without form.** Multi-stakeholder optimisation outside urban design assigns objectives to stakeholders and selects from the Pareto set a posteriori (Herman et al., 2014; Kaim et al., 2020). Game-theoretic models of Chinese renewal represent government, developers and residents as conflicting parties (Liu et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2024), and serious games distribute spatial decisions among players (Nourian et al., 2024); none produces building-level morphology. What is new here is therefore not the Pareto set or the a posteriori choice, but that the generator's degrees of freedom are distributed among classes holding the same existing stock — and that the consequence of that distribution is measured before and after search (Table S1 positions the paper against these strands).

## 3. Case and data

The study area is the Dapuqiao subdistrict in Huangpu District, centred on Tianzifang, where shikumen lilong and converted factories carry housing, shops and cultural uses in the same blocks (Figure 1). Building footprints, heights, uses, the street network and protected-building records were combined into a model of 1,370 buildings on a 1,573,924 m² site, with a total gross floor area of 4,440,344 m². Street-bounded blocks define 38 renewal units, which organise operators and constraint checks; a unit holds 36 buildings on average.

**Labels.** Each building receives one stakeholder class by a cascade of three sources — parcel land use, then building function, then place-name keywords — the first source that matches decides, and buildings matched by none remain unclassified (the full rule is Table S2). The resulting distribution (Table 1) is the input to every permission table: 780 resident-held buildings (56.9%) carrying 48.9% of the floor area, 344 developer/operator-held (25.1%, 31.8%), 183 public assets (13.4%, 17.6%) and 63 unclassified (4.6%, 1.7%). Twenty-one footprints matched to official heritage records are frozen in every group and carry 0.73% of the floor area; 32 buildings that could not be stably matched to a renewal unit are held unchanged and carry 0.82%.

## 4. Method

### 4.1 What this study measures

The object of measurement is not a form but a permission table. For a table *T*, fixed premises *R* and a fixed objective vector *f*, the instrument returns five quantities, and every table in this paper is reported on all five.

1. **Ceiling** — the floor area (or released ground) that *T* makes attainable when one objective is pushed to its limit and the others are ignored, computed analytically before search and subject to the same premises. Because it depends only on *T* and *R*, it separates what a table allows from what a search finds.
2. **Reach** — the non-dominated objective vectors *T* makes attainable, summarised by hypervolume in a common normalised space.
3. **Price** — the difference between that reach and the reach of the least attributed table compatible with *R*, reported as a hypervolume ratio and as the share of a group's solutions dominated by the unattributed group's merged front.
4. **Attribution** — whether reach and price follow from *which* buildings a table permits rather than from *how much* it permits, tested against a null model that keeps the quantity of permission and discards its attribution.
5. **Exchange** — gross additions against gross removals for every solution, because a table that lets one class build and another cede cannot be summarised by a net figure alone.

These five quantities also define what would count as a failure of the instrument, which is worth stating before the results. It fails if the null model reproduces a scenario's signature (then attribution adds nothing); if the searched maximum sits at the ceiling for every group (then search, not governance, was the binding limit); or if the ordering of role picks does not survive relabelling and a change of exposure scale (then the result is an artefact of the proxies). Section 5 reports each of these checks rather than assuming the favourable outcome.

### 4.2 Two layers of authority, and the envelope actually applied

The framework separates premises that the user sets and the run does not negotiate from levers that distinguish governance scenarios (Figure 2). Premises are the regulatory envelope, the stakeholder labels and the heritage freeze. Levers are the permission tables: which operators may act on which classes, at what intensity, and over what maximum share of the stock. Role priorities act only after generation. Every input is a published table, so a reader can edit any premise or lever and rerun.

**The envelope is a non-regression envelope, and this has to be said plainly.** Dapuqiao is not a low-rise district: 423 of 1,370 buildings (30.9%) already stand at or above 36 m, and the mean unit plot ratio is 3.04 (median 2.80; area-weighted 2.82). A flat 36 m limit and a flat plot ratio of 3.0 would declare a third of the existing city illegal. The envelope is therefore written as a floor on the existing condition and a ceiling on the proposed one: a building may not exceed max(36 m, its current height); a renewal unit may not exceed max(3.0, its current plot ratio); coverage may not exceed max(0.60, its current coverage); and residential floor area may not fall below 80% of its current value. The four nominal values are research assumptions of this study, not statutory plot-level controls (Section 6.3); their sources are recorded in Table S3.

Two consequences follow, and they are reported as findings rather than buried. Sixteen of the 38 units already sit at or above a plot ratio of 3.0, so no densification is available in them at all; and for the 423 buildings at or above 36 m the height headroom is zero. Combining the two filters with the permission tables gives the real action space of this district: of 337 developer-held buildings, only **54, spread over 6 of the 38 units**, can gain any height under the strictest reading, and only 585 buildings of any class can gain height if no label is read. This is the structural reason why differences between governance settings are small in absolute terms, and it is measured, not assumed.

### 4.3 Governance scenarios and operators

Six operators change height, footprint or volume (Table 2). Each is a rule with an applicable building class, a mode of change and an intensity. Ground release, for example, shrinks a footprint and then raises height within the envelope to recover part of the floor area, so the same operation can free ground at the street and raise exposure in the block. The three scenarios differ only in which class may use which operator and how strongly; protected buildings are untouched in all of them, and unclassified buildings receive no operator in any table.

The intensity column changes how the comparison must be read. Public coordination is not "development growth with fewer operators": development growth gives developers densification and splitting at full strength but a **lower** ground-release intensity (0.55 against 0.75), and it never permits the height redistribution that both other scenarios allow. Across the 38 units this produces a markedly different action space per scenario: densification is available in 6 units under public coordination and development growth and in none under resident-and-heritage priority; ground release in 15; volume splitting in 13; height redistribution in 12; public-asset setback in 13; courtyard access in 28.

### 4.4 Constraints and objectives

Every candidate must satisfy hard constraints on geometric validity, the study boundary, the heritage freeze, the height envelope, unit plot ratio, unit coverage, residential retention, new overlaps and, per scenario, the maximum share of buildings changed (18%, 30% and 14% of the stock in the three scenarios; 30%, 45% and 22% of the units). Intensities are first reduced to fit the remaining constraint margin, and each reduction is attributed to the rule that caused it (`height_limit`, `zone_far`, `footprint_minimum`, `intensity_cap`); candidates that still violate a constraint are discarded, and the violating rule is logged. Each operation is logged with building, unit, operator, intensity and before/after attributes. Three objectives are shared by all five groups.

**Residential exposure (minimise)** is the share of residential floor area that is altered, or lies in a renewal unit where another building gains height or volume:

 f₁ = Σ_{i∈R} GFA_i · I_i / Σ_{i∈R} GFA_i ,  I_i = 1 if *i* is modified, or shares a unit with a building that is densified, split or receives redistributed height   (1)

where *R* is the set of resident-held buildings. Because units hold about 36 buildings, one intervention can expose a whole block; the measure is deliberately conservative, and Section 5.5 recomputes it with distance thresholds instead of units.

**Net floor-area change (maximise)** is the change in gross floor area across the study area, so that removals count against additions:

 f₂ = ( Σ_i GFA_i^prop − Σ_i GFA_i^base ) / A_site   (2)

with gross additions Σ_i max(0, GFA_i^prop − GFA_i^base) and gross removals Σ_i max(0, GFA_i^base − GFA_i^prop) reported separately for every solution. The objective is the net figure; the two components are what makes "who builds and who gives up" readable, because a table that permits one class to add and another to cede cannot be summarised by its net alone. Values are reported in per cent of the site area and can be negative.

**Street-adjacent ground release (maximise)** is the footprint area removed within 5 m of a street, normalised by the area of that buffer:

 f₃ = area( (F_base \ F_prop) ∩ S₅ ) / area( S₅ ∩ B )   (3)

where *F* is the union of footprints, *S₅* the 5 m street buffer and *B* the study boundary. The buffer covers 246,967 m², 15.7% of the site, so f₃ is read as a share of the ground that could actually be released to a street, not as a share of the district.

One property of the objective set should be declared rather than discovered by a reviewer: because every footprint operator is subtractive, unit coverage can only fall, so the coverage premise cannot bind. It is retained because a reader expects to see it in a regulatory envelope, and Section 5.5 reports its activity as zero.

### 4.5 Search and role selection

Each group is searched with NSGA-II (Deb et al., 2002). The genome is fixed-length and unit-based: for each of the 38 units, one integer selecting an operator from those available in that unit (including "no change") and one real intensity in [0, 1], giving a 76-dimensional mixed vector. Availability is computed before the search: an operator appears for a unit only if the unit contains a building the table makes eligible **and** the operator can still produce a change there — which is why units at their plot-ratio ceiling never receive densification. Duplicate genomes reuse cached evaluations. Populations of ⟨E0: N = 40⟩ run for ⟨E0: G = 50⟩ generations, giving about 2,000 independent evaluations per run, over ⟨E0: k = 10⟩ paired random seeds; convergence is checked by hypervolume against a common reference point fixed before the runs (Zitzler & Thiele, 1999). Near-identical solutions are merged with per-objective ε-tolerances of 0.001, 0.0005 and 0.0003 for f₁, f₂ and f₃ — the last one re-derived so that the same relative resolution is kept after f₃ changed denominator. ⟨E0: measured single-evaluation cost on the frozen input⟩

Three role perspectives then rank each Pareto set lexicographically, moving to the next objective when the difference on the current one is within ε (Kaim et al., 2020): residents order exposure, then net floor area, then ground release; development orders net floor area, ground release, exposure; public planning orders ground release, exposure, net floor area. A compromise solution minimises the worst rank across the three roles. The orders are the authors' stylised readings of each role, not elicited preferences.

### 4.6 The five comparison groups

Five groups share input, premises, objectives and evaluation budget (Table 3). Groups 2–4 are the attributed tables; group 5 is the unattributed reference against which the other three are measured, and it is listed last because it is the object this study adds to the four the district already contains or the framework generates.

**Table 3.** The five groups.

| # | Group | Permission table | Role in the comparison |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Existing conditions | none; no building changes | reference point; fixes the zero of f₁, f₂, f₃ |
| 2 | Public coordination | Table 2, column 4 | attributed permission, coordination logic |
| 3 | Development growth | Table 2, column 5 | attributed permission, growth logic |
| 4 | Resident + heritage priority | Table 2, column 6 | attributed permission, continuity logic |
| 5 | Unattributed parametric optimum | label-blind: every non-heritage building, height ×1.00–1.30 (A1: ×1.00–1.50), footprint retention 0.75–1.00 (A1: 0.65–1.00) | the best reachable when authority is not attributed; the envelope that bounds groups 2–4 |

Group 5 is a group rather than a baseline because it is searched with the same algorithm, budget, objectives and premises, and appears on the same axes: it is the same generator with the label term switched off. Its signature is one-directional — it raises heights and shrinks footprints, so it removes floor area only as a by-product of releasing ground — whereas groups 2–4 remove floor area deliberately, in buildings whose class the table permits to cede. That asymmetry is the substance of the comparison, not an artefact of the parameterisation.

**Amplitude arms.** Two settings are run for every group. Arm A0 keeps the previous amplitudes (height gain 30%, footprint reduction 25%, gap 8%, redistribution 30%; multipliers 1.00–1.30 and 0.75–1.00) so that results stay comparable with the earlier run. Arm A1 widens them (50%, 35%, 15%, 50%; multipliers 1.00–1.50 and 0.65–1.00), because the previous amplitudes left most of the regulatory headroom unused. The parametric multipliers are widened in step on purpose: otherwise group 5 would no longer bound groups 2–4 and the logic of Section 5.1 would collapse. The difference between A0 and A1 measures how much of the previous "small effect" was an amplitude artefact rather than a governance effect. ⟨E0: which arm is primary, fixed after both runs⟩

**Null model.** For each scenario, K = ⟨E1: 10⟩ random permission tables are drawn that preserve, per operator, the number of eligible buildings and their total floor area within ±5%, but ignore the labels; tables are stratified by renewal unit so the spatial distribution of permission is not skewed. Each table is searched with the same budget over three of the E0 seeds. If a scenario's reach and price fall inside the null distribution, its morphological signature follows from the amount of permission rather than from its attribution, and Section 5.3 says so.

**Statistics.** Hypervolumes are computed in a common normalised space against a reference point fixed before the runs, and compared across paired seeds with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, Holm-corrected across the three comparisons of groups 2–4 against group 5, and reported with effect sizes, directions, median differences and the number of seeds agreeing in direction. Ten seeds are used because five cannot support the test: with five pairs the smallest two-sided p-value is 0.0625 and, after Holm correction, 0.1875, so a five-seed design cannot reject at conventional levels whatever the effect; with ten pairs the floor falls to 0.00195 and 0.0059 respectively.

### 4.7 Professional evaluation

Following toolkit-evaluation practice, the evaluation is a demonstration across the configuration space rather than a usage study (Ledo et al., 2018). Because the instrument's claims are about legibility — whether a reader can see who may change what, and what it costs — a small professional evaluation was added alongside it. Three to five professionals (planning, architecture, heritage conservation, development) are invited to use the deployed interface for about fifteen minutes with one task: compare the five groups, choose a solution for a role of their own choosing, and justify it. The interface is deployed as a static page so that participants can be recruited independently of the authors, and it records an event log (group and role switches, solutions opened, time per step, exports). Participants answer four questions and one open question (Table S4): whether they can tell which class may change what, whether they can see what a group's setting costs and who pays, whether the per-building record is useful for judging a scheme, and what is missing for use in a real negotiation. This is a professional evaluation of interpretability with n = 3–5: it is not a usability study, it is not statistically powered, and it is reported as such.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 What each permission table makes attainable

The ceiling comes first, because it needs no search and because it already contains most of the paper's argument. Pushing one objective to its limit and ignoring the other two, the addable envelope contracts sharply once permission is attributed (Table 4, ceiling columns). If every class may densify — group 5's table — the district can add 14.83% of its site area in arm A0 and 17.72% in A1, while still respecting every unit's plot-ratio headroom and the 30% cap on changed buildings. Confined to developer-held buildings, which is what all three governance tables permit, the same operator can add 1.72% (A0) or 2.50% (A1) under development growth, 0.80% or 1.31% under public coordination, and nothing at all under resident-and-heritage priority. **Attribution, in other words, removes seven to eighteen times the addable envelope before the optimiser is switched on**, and it does so through a chain that can be inspected: 337 eligible buildings become 54 with headroom, spread over 6 of 38 units.

The release objective is even more tightly bounded, and this is the answer to a question the earlier draft could not answer — why the scenarios' maximum ground release looked almost identical. The ceiling of f₃ is 0.38–0.50% of the street buffer for the attributed groups and 2.13–2.76% for the unattributed one, and the ceiling is not free: reaching it costs 10–19% of the district's floor area in the attributed groups (`open_ground` shrinks footprints and can only partly compensate height under the 36 m envelope) and 74–102% in the unattributed group. Release and net floor area are therefore in violent conflict near the boundary of the feasible set, which is exactly why the searched fronts stay close to the origin rather than marching along f₃.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 What the instrument computes and what this run shows

Two claims should be kept apart. The instrument's claim is general: given any permission table, labelling and set of premises, it computes the trade-offs that table makes available, the ceiling those trade-offs cannot exceed, and the buildings that carry them. The run's claims are conditional on the tables used here. Some follow directly from them: development growth adds the most floor area because it grants developers the most operators at full intensity, and in an attributed scenario the residents' pick has zero exposure because exposure is their first criterion. These are checks of correct construction, not findings. The findings are what the tables do not dictate: the contraction of the addable envelope under attribution (5.1), the low-exposure region that permissions reach and the unattributed optimum does not (5.2), whether attribution matters beyond quantity (5.3), how much of the ceiling the search actually finds, and whether any of it survives a change of labels and exposure scale (5.5).

### 6.2 What is gained and what is threatened

Writing governance as permissions makes the distribution of costs legible: in every attributed scenario, floor area gained by development is paid for in residential exposure, and solutions preferred from a residents' perspective remove ⟨E0⟩ m² of floor area held by other classes. Reporting gross additions and removals separately is what makes that exchange inspectable; a net figure alone would let a table that removes from residents and adds for operators look like a table that did nothing. The same legibility carries risks. Role orders are the authors' readings of residents, operators and planners; presenting them as those groups' choices would ventriloquise them. A permission table that protects residents can equally be read as a licence for everything it does not forbid, and codifying "who may change what" can lend a computational air of neutrality to decisions that, in Shanghai, are made through state-led land and conservation mandates (Wu, 2018). Labels drawn from land use flatten Tianzifang's resident-landlords, who are claimants on rent as well as parties exposed to change (Arkaraprasertkul, 2018). Heritage enters only as a freeze, which treats conservation as uncontested, although conservation and displacement are entangled in this district (Shin, 2016).

### 6.3 Limitations and next steps

Renewal units are street blocks, not statutory plots; the envelope is a non-regression envelope built from the existing condition rather than from plot-level control plans, and the discrepancy matters most in the 16 units already at their plot-ratio ceiling, where the model concludes that nothing can be added. The four nominal regulatory values are research assumptions. Four heritage records remain unverified. Objectives are proxies: exposure is not wellbeing, and ground release is not usable public space without tenure, access and management data. Two amplitude settings were run because the previous study's amplitudes left most headroom unused, and the residual gap between the searched maximum and the permission ceiling is not resolved here; both bound how strongly the reach of any single group should be read. The professional evaluation is a review of interpretability with n = 3–5, not a usage study, and cannot support claims about how parties would negotiate. The next step is to hand the permission tables to people rather than to authors: in a workshop with residents, operators, planners and conservation officers, each party would hold its own row of the table, the generator would render the district after every round, and the precomputed fronts would serve as a referee showing how far a negotiated settlement falls from the efficient set (Nourian et al., 2024).

---

## 7. Conclusion

This paper represents renewal governance as permission over morphological operators and stakeholder preference as an a posteriori ordering over a shared Pareto set, and applies the pair to 1,370 buildings in Shanghai's Dapuqiao district. It is not a design tool; it is an instrument that measures what a distribution of authority costs. Because permissions are attributed, the addable envelope contracts from 14.8–17.7% of the site to 0.8–2.5% before any search, and the releasable envelope to 0.3–0.5% of the street buffer at a cost of 10–19% of the district's floor area; against these ceilings, attributed permissions reach ⟨E6⟩ and concede ⟨E0⟩ of the attainable objective space, and the permission-matched null model ⟨E1: reproduces / does not reproduce⟩ their signature. Because every permission, operation and selection is recorded per building, the framework turns "who may change what, and who pays" from an assumption into an output that can be inspected, contested and rerun.
