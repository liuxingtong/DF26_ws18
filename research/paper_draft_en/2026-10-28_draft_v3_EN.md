# WHO MAY CHANGE WHAT
## Governance permissions as morphological operators for multi-stakeholder renewal in Shanghai's Dapuqiao district

REMOVED FOR PEER REVIEW

**Abstract.** This paper is not a design tool, and it is deliberately not a parametric toy. It is a measurement instrument, and what it measures is a distribution of authority. Renewal of historic districts is decided less by what a design optimises than by who is permitted to alter which buildings: generative tools expose form or performance parameters to a single user, and participatory tools collect preferences over predetermined options, but neither represents how transformation authority over an existing building stock is distributed. We encode that distribution directly and then measure its price. In Shanghai's Dapuqiao (Tianzifang) district, 1,370 buildings are attributed to four stakeholder classes — 780 resident-held, 344 developer/operator-held, 183 public assets and 63 unclassified — and three governance scenarios are written as permission tables that bind six morphological operators to particular classes of building under one shared regulatory envelope. Five groups are compared under identical inputs, objectives and constraints: the district with nothing changed; a no-stakeholder parametric optimum searched without reading any label; and the three governance scenarios. Each is searched with NSGA-II against three shared objectives (residential exposure, net floor-area change and street-adjacent ground release), with gross additions and gross removals reported alongside the net figure, and three role perspectives then select from the resulting Pareto sets a posteriori. We measure three things: how much of the technically attainable envelope each permission table gives up, whether the attribution of permission matters beyond its quantity, and whether either survives relabelling, a change of exposure scale and a change of tolerance. ⟨E0/E1: one-sentence headline result⟩ The contribution is an auditable two-stage mapping (who may change what, then how to choose) that separates what the instrument can compute from what a particular run shows, and makes the cost of each governance setting legible at building resolution.

**Keywords.** Urban renewal; stakeholder governance; multi-objective optimisation; a posteriori preferences; heritage districts; research instrument.

## 1. Introduction

Shanghai has turned its renewal policy from demolition-led redevelopment to retention: the principle of "retain, renovate, demolish" was codified in the Shanghai Urban Renewal Regulations (Standing Committee of the Shanghai Municipal People's Congress, 2021), and the master plan caps total construction land, so new capacity must come from the existing stock. Under these conditions the decisive question in a historic district is no longer what to build on cleared land but who may transform which existing building, by how much, and at whose expense. Tianzifang, the lilong-and-factory core of the Dapuqiao subdistrict, makes the question concrete. Its spatial evolution has been read as the product of power relations among the district government, operators and residents (Wang & Zhou, 2022); its original residents have become landlords who capture rent from heritage (Arkaraprasertkul, 2018); and the district's management rules require at once the protection of characteristic buildings, the improvement of community facilities and the coordination of commercial and residential uses (Huangpu District People's Government, 2023).

Computational urban design has tools for conflicting objectives but not for conflicting claimants. Evolutionary multi-objective methods return a Pareto set of alternatives (Koenig et al., 2020), yet the objectives belong to no one, and the field's own systematic review names limited stakeholder integration as a standing gap (Jiang et al., 2024). Participatory tools bring many people in, but mostly as assessors of predetermined options, rarely beyond consultation (Hanan & Carhart, 2025; Goncalves et al., 2026). A tool that lets a user adjust parameters and compare scenarios does not by itself represent stakeholders, however its sliders are labelled.

**What this paper is and is not.** This paper does not propose a deeper design tool, and it is not a parametric toy that a designer plays with until a form appears. It proposes a narrow, explicit instrument: a way of writing down who may change what, and of measuring what that distribution costs. Its output is not a preferred scheme but a set of commensurable quantities — how much of the attainable envelope a permission table concedes, where it reaches configurations that unattributed parameters cannot, and how much of the result survives changes in the labels and the measures. The instrument is therefore judged by whether those quantities are computed correctly and reported honestly, not by whether its forms are attractive.

We argue that stakeholders differ from users not in what they prefer but in what they can claim. Classes of actors hold, or are taken to hold, different parts of the same building stock, and a permission granted to one class is exercised on buildings whose effects fall on others: a developer's densification raises exposure for residents in the same block. We therefore represent governance before generation, as permission over morphological operators (which classes' buildings may be altered, by which operators and at what intensity), and preferences after generation, as role-specific orderings over a shared Pareto set (Kaim et al., 2020). The model represents the authority to transform buildings under given regulations, not the authority to set those regulations; the latter enters as fixed, user-editable premises (Section 4.2). Earlier work by the authors read stakeholder power through a height-only transform across several districts (REMOVED FOR PEER REVIEW); this paper instead couples permissions to operator-level form generation and multi-objective search on a single heritage site.

**Research question and design.** The question is whether assigning transformation authority to stakeholder classes produces trade-offs that a parameter tool working under the same constraints cannot reach, and that the same quantity of permission assigned without regard to who holds the buildings does not reproduce. Answering it requires a comparison, not a demonstration: five groups are searched under identical data, objectives and constraints — the existing district with nothing changed, a no-stakeholder parametric optimum, and the three governance scenarios (Sections 4.1 and 4.6).

The contributions are (1) an auditable two-stage mapping from governance permissions to form and from role priorities to selection, recorded building by building; (2) a measurement design that separates what the instrument can compute from what this run shows — a five-group comparison, a permission ceiling derived from each table, a label-blind parametric optimum, a permission-matched null model, and robustness tests on labels, exposure scale and tolerances; and (3) a reading of the Dapuqiao results in terms of who bears which part of each trade-off.

## 2. Related work

**Objectives without owners.** Procedural and evolutionary generators turn designer-set targets into form and resolve contradictory goals as non-dominated sets (Koenig et al., 2020; Miao et al., 2020). Metric-to-form mappings make morphology indicators the entire specification of a block (Cai et al., 2025), and interactive generators let a designer explore alternatives from a handful of parameters (Talmor Blaistain & Fisher-Gewirtzman, 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). In all of these the quantities that renewal politics fights over (intensity, coverage, height) are set, but not attributed to anyone.

**Preferences without claims.** Participatory systems collect votes, comments or mapped preferences; the closest CAADRIA precedent lets citizens propose and vote on heritage interventions, while noting that citizens' power over the decision remains limited (Leong & Janssen, 2022). Reviews against Arnstein's (1969) ladder find most digital tools at consultation (Hanan & Carhart, 2025). Where values are elicited from citizens, they are often found to lie outside the design space being explored (Knecht et al., 2019); simulated resident agents optimise satisfaction rather than represent parties to a decision (Zhou et al., 2024).

**Claims without form.** Multi-stakeholder optimisation outside urban design assigns objectives to stakeholders and selects from the Pareto set a posteriori (Herman et al., 2014; Kaim et al., 2020). Game-theoretic models of Chinese renewal represent government, developers and residents as conflicting parties (Liu et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2024), and serious games distribute spatial decisions among players (Nourian et al., 2024); none of these produces building-level morphology. Table 1 positions the paper: what is new is not the Pareto set or the a posteriori choice, but that the generator's degrees of freedom are distributed among classes holding the same existing stock, and that the distribution itself is the object of measurement.

**Table 1.** Positioning against existing approaches.

| Approach | What is set | Held by | Output | Conflicting claims on existing stock |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| **This paper** | **operator permissions per class** | **stakeholder classes** | **forms + building log + measured price of the table** | **yes, at building resolution** |

## 3. Case and data

The study area is the Dapuqiao subdistrict in Huangpu District, centred on Tianzifang, where shikumen lilong and converted factories carry housing, shops and cultural uses in the same blocks (Figure 1). Building footprints, heights, uses, the street network and protected-building records were combined into a model of 1,370 buildings with a total gross floor area of 4.44 million m² on 1.57 km² of land. Street-bounded blocks define 38 renewal units, which organise operators and constraint checks; the units hold 36 buildings on average, so a single intervention can expose a whole block. Thirty-two buildings that could not be stably matched to a unit are held unchanged. Twenty-one footprints matched to official heritage records are frozen; four further records await verification of their extent and are excluded from the frozen set. Areas and distances are computed in a projected coordinate system, and a single frozen version of the input is used for every group in every run.

**Stakeholder labels.** Each building receives exactly one class through a three-source cascade in which the first source that yields a mapping decides (Tables 2 and 3): land-use class, then building-level function, then place-name keywords; buildings that match none are left unclassified. The labels are a modelling assumption about who may act on a building, not a survey of tenure, and they are deliberately coarse: land use is a weak proxy for control in Tianzifang in particular, where many residential buildings host ground-floor commerce let by residents. The distribution (Table 3) is therefore reported in full, including the unclassified residue, and Section 5.5 repeats the analysis under an alternative labelling and under controlled misclassification.

## 4. Method

### 4.1 What this study measures

The object of measurement is not a form but a permission table. For a table T, fixed premises R and a fixed objective vector f, the instrument returns three quantities. **Reach** is the set of non-dominated objective vectors that T makes attainable under R, and its hypervolume. **Price** is the difference between that reach and the reach of the least attributed table compatible with R — group 2, in which every non-heritage building may move by the declared parametric ranges — reported as a hypervolume ratio and as the share of a group's solutions that the group-2 envelope dominates. **Attribution** is whether reach and price follow from *which* buildings the table permits rather than from *how much* it permits, tested against a null model that keeps the quantity of permission and discards its attribution.

Two further quantities keep the comparison honest. A **permission ceiling** is computed before any search: the floor area that could be added if every building the table makes eligible were pushed to its individual and unit limits while the other two objectives were ignored. It is a property of the table and of R, not of the search, so comparing a searched maximum with it separates "what the table allows" from "what the search found". And every solution is reported with **gross additions and gross removals** as well as their net, because a table that lets classes both build and cede cannot be summarised by a single net number.

The five groups share input, premises, objectives and budget (Table 5). The comparison answers three questions in order: what does each table cost relative to the attainable envelope (5.1); does the attribution of permission matter (5.2–5.3); and does either answer survive a change of labels, measure and tolerance (5.5).

### 4.2 Two layers of authority

The framework separates premises that the user sets and the run does not negotiate from levers that distinguish governance scenarios (Figure 2). Premises are the regulatory envelope, the stakeholder labels and the heritage freeze. Levers are the permission tables: which operators may act on which classes of building, at what intensity, and over what maximum share of the stock. Role priorities act only after generation. Every input is a published table, so a reader can edit any premise or lever and rerun.

**The regulatory envelope is a non-regression envelope, and this must be stated plainly.** Dapuqiao is not a low-rise district: 423 of 1,370 buildings (31%) already stand at or above 36 m, and the mean existing plot ratio is 3.04. Applying a flat 36 m limit and a flat FAR of 3.0 would declare a third of the existing city illegal. The envelope is therefore written as a floor on the existing condition and a ceiling on the proposed one. A building may not exceed max(36 m, its current height); a renewal unit may not exceed max(3.0, its current plot ratio); coverage may not exceed max(0.60, its current coverage); and residential floor area may not fall below 80% of its current value. Two consequences follow and are reported as findings rather than hidden: 16 of the 38 units already sit at or above a plot ratio of 3.0, so no densification is available in them at all; and for the 423 buildings already at or above 36 m the height headroom is zero. The values 36 m, 3.0, 0.60 and 0.80 are research assumptions of this study, not statutory plot-level controls (Section 6.3); ⟨E3: source of each value, e.g. control-plan technical guideline or research assumption⟩.

### 4.3 Governance scenarios and operators

Six operators change height, footprint or volume (Table 4, Figure 4). Each is a rule with an applicable building class, a mode of change and an intensity. Ground release, for example, shrinks a footprint and then raises height within the limits to recover part of the floor area, so the same operation can free ground at the street and raise exposure in the block. The three scenarios differ only in which class may use which operator and how strongly; protected and unclassified buildings are untouched in all of them.

### 4.4 Constraints and objectives

Every candidate must satisfy hard constraints on geometric validity, the study boundary, the heritage freeze, the height envelope, unit plot ratio, unit coverage, residential retention, new overlaps and, per scenario, the maximum share of buildings changed. Intensities are first reduced to fit the remaining constraint margin; candidates that still violate a constraint are discarded, and the constraint that did so is logged. Each operation is logged with building, unit, operator, intensity and before/after attributes. Three objectives are shared by all five groups.

Residential exposure (minimise) is the share of residential floor area that is altered or lies in a renewal unit where another building gains height or volume:

 f₁ = Σ_{i∈R} GFA_i · I_i / Σ_{i∈R} GFA_i ,   I_i = 1 if i is modified, or shares a unit with a building that is densified, split or receives redistributed height (1)

where R is the set of resident-held buildings. Because units hold about 36 buildings, one intervention can expose a whole block; the measure is deliberately conservative, and Section 5.5 recomputes it with distance thresholds instead of units.

Net floor-area change (maximise) is the change in gross floor area across the study area, so that removals count against additions:

 f₂ = ( Σ_i GFA_i^prop − Σ_i GFA_i^base ) / A_site (2)

with gross additions Σ_i max(0, GFA_i^prop − GFA_i^base) and gross removals Σ_i max(0, GFA_i^base − GFA_i^prop) reported separately for every solution. The net figure is the objective; the two components are what makes "who builds and who gives up" readable, because a table that permits one class to add and another to cede cannot be summarised by its net alone. Values are expressed in per cent of the site area, and can be negative.

Street-adjacent ground release (maximise) is the footprint area removed within 5 m of a street, normalised by the area of that buffer:

 f₃ = area( (F_base \ F_prop) ∩ S₅) / area(S₅ ∩ B) (3)

where F is the union of footprints, S₅ the 5 m street buffer and B the study boundary. The buffer covers 246,967 m², 15.7% of the site, so f₃ is read as a share of the ground that could actually be released to a street, not as a share of the whole district.

One property of the objective set should be declared rather than discovered by a reviewer: because every footprint operator is subtractive, unit coverage can only fall, so the coverage premise cannot bind. It is retained because it is part of the regulatory envelope a reader expects to see, and Section 5.5 reports its activity as zero.

### 4.5 Search and role selection

Each group is searched with NSGA-II (Deb et al., 2002). The genome is fixed-length and unit-based: for each of the 38 renewal units, one integer selecting an operator from the set available in that unit (including "no change") and one real intensity in [0, 1], giving a 76-dimensional mixed vector. Availability is unit-level and computed before the search: an operator appears only if the unit contains a building the scenario's table makes eligible **and** the operator can still produce a change there, which is why units at their plot-ratio ceiling never receive densification. The number of units that may be changed is capped per scenario. Duplicate genomes reuse cached evaluations. Populations of ⟨E0: N = 40⟩ run for ⟨E0: G = 50⟩ generations, giving about 2,000 unique evaluations per run, over ⟨E0: k = 10⟩ paired random seeds; convergence is checked by hypervolume against a common reference point (Zitzler & Thiele, 1999). Near-identical solutions are merged with a per-objective ε-tolerance of 0.001, 0.0005 and 0.00005 for f₁, f₂ and f₃ respectively, the f₂ tolerance being re-derived for the net definition. ⟨E0: single-evaluation cost, measured on the frozen input⟩

Three role perspectives then rank each Pareto set lexicographically, moving to the next objective when the difference on the current one is within ε (Kaim et al., 2020): residents order exposure, then net floor area, then ground release; development orders net floor area, ground release, exposure; public planning orders ground release, exposure, net floor area. A compromise solution minimises the worst rank across the three roles. The orders are the authors' stylised readings of each role, not elicited preferences.

Group 2 is a group rather than a baseline because it is searched with the same algorithm, budget, objectives and premises, and appears on the same axes: it is the same generator with the label term switched off. Its signature is one-directional — it raises heights and shrinks footprints but cannot remove floor area in one place to add it in another — whereas groups 3–5, whose permissions are exercised by classes on different buildings, both add and remove. That asymmetry is the substance of the comparison, not an artefact of the parameterisation.

**Amplitude arms.** Two settings are run for every group. Arm A0 keeps the previous amplitudes (height gain 30%, footprint reduction 25%, gap 8%, redistribution 30%; parametric multipliers 1.00–1.30 and 0.75–1.00) so that results stay comparable with the earlier run. Arm A1 widens them (50%, 35%, 15%, 50%; multipliers 1.00–1.50 and 0.65–1.00) because the previous amplitudes left most of the regulatory headroom unused. The parametric multipliers are widened in step on purpose: otherwise group 2 would no longer bound groups 3–5 and the logic of Section 5.1 would collapse. The difference between A0 and A1 measures how much of the previous "small effect" was an amplitude artefact rather than a governance effect. ⟨E0: which arm is primary, to be fixed after both runs⟩

**Null model.** For each scenario, K = ⟨E1: 10⟩ random permission tables are drawn that preserve, per operator, the number of eligible buildings and their total floor area within ±5%, but ignore the labels; tables are stratified by renewal unit so that the spatial distribution of permission is not skewed. Each table is searched with the same budget over three of the E0 seeds. If the scenario's reach and price fall inside the null distribution, then the morphological signature follows from the amount of permission rather than from its attribution, and Section 5.3 says so.

**Statistics.** Hypervolumes are computed in a common normalised space against a single reference point fixed before the runs, and compared across paired seeds with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, Holm-corrected across the three comparisons of groups 3–5 against group 2, and reported with effect sizes, directions, median differences and the number of seeds agreeing in direction. Ten seeds are used because five cannot support the test: with five pairs the smallest two-sided p-value is 0.0625, and after Holm correction the floor is 0.1875, so a five-seed design cannot reject at conventional levels whatever the effect.

### 4.7 Professional evaluation

Following toolkit-evaluation practice, the evaluation is a technical demonstration across the configuration space rather than a usage study (Ledo et al., 2018). Because the instrument's claims are about legibility — whether a reader can see who may change what, and what it costs — a small structured review was added. Three to five professionals (planning, architecture, heritage conservation, development) were asked to use the deployed interface for about fifteen minutes with one task: compare the five groups, choose a solution for a role of their own choosing, and justify it. The interface records an event log (group and role switches, solutions opened, time per step, exports), and participants answered ⟨EV: 4⟩ short questions and one open question (Table 10). This is an expert review of interpretability with n = 3–5; it is not a usability study, it is not statistically powered, and it is reported as such.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 What each group can reach, and what it costs

The permission ceiling is the first result, because it separates the table from the search. Before any run, the no-stakeholder table allows at most ⟨E0: ceiling, arm A1⟩ of additional floor area, and the three governance tables allow ⟨E0: PC / DG / RH ceilings⟩, computed as the geometric maximum with the other two objectives ignored. Two structural facts explain most of the gap between the groups: 16 of 38 units are at or above a plot ratio of 3.0 and therefore admit no densification at all, and 423 buildings already at or above 36 m have no height headroom. Counting densification alone and ignoring the other two objectives, the A0 ceiling is 15.5% of the site area; widening the amplitude to A1 raises it to 18.7%, a 21% relative gain and not a transformation.

In the previous run the searched maxima sat well below the corresponding ceilings (Figure 3, Table 6). ## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 What the instrument computes and what this run shows

Two claims should be kept apart. The instrument's claim is general: given any permission table, labelling and set of premises, it computes the trade-offs that table makes available, the ceiling those trade-offs cannot exceed, and the buildings that carry them. The run's claims are conditional on the tables used here. Some follow directly from them: development growth adds the most floor area because it grants developers the most operators at full intensity, and in an attributed scenario the residents' pick has zero exposure because exposure is their first criterion. These are checks of correct construction, not findings. The findings are what the tables do not dictate: the low-exposure region that permissions reach and the parametric optimum does not (5.2), whether attribution matters beyond quantity (5.3), how much of the possible change the search finds (5.1), and whether any of it survives a change of labels and exposure scale (5.5).

### 6.2 What is gained and what is threatened

Writing governance as permissions makes the distribution of costs legible: in every attributed scenario, the floor area gained by development is paid for in residential exposure, and the solutions preferred from a residents' perspective remove ⟨E0⟩ m² of floor area held by other classes. Reporting gross additions and removals separately is what makes that exchange inspectable; a net figure alone would let a table that removes from residents and adds for operators look like a table that did nothing. The same legibility carries risks. The role orders are the authors' readings of residents, operators and planners; presenting them as those groups' choices would ventriloquise them. A permission table that protects residents can equally be read as a licence for everything it does not forbid, and codifying "who may change what" can lend a computational air of neutrality to decisions that, in Shanghai, are made through state-led land and conservation mandates (Wu, 2018). Labels drawn from land use flatten Tianzifang's resident-landlords, who are claimants on rent as well as parties exposed to change (Arkaraprasertkul, 2018). Heritage enters only as a freeze, which treats conservation as uncontested, although conservation and displacement are entangled in this district (Shin, 2016).

### 6.3 Limitations and next steps

Renewal units are street blocks, not statutory plots; the envelope is a non-regression envelope built from the existing condition rather than from plot-level control plans, and the discrepancy matters in the 16 units already at their plot-ratio ceiling, where the model concludes that nothing can be added. The four regulatory values are research assumptions. Four heritage records remain unverified. Objectives are proxies: exposure is not wellbeing, and ground release is not usable public space without tenure, access and management data. Two amplitude settings were run because the previous study's amplitudes left most headroom unused; the residual gap between the searched maximum and the permission ceiling is not resolved here and bounds how strongly the reach of any single group should be read. The professional evaluation is an expert review of interpretability with n = 3–5, not a usage study, and cannot support claims about how parties would negotiate. The next step is to hand the permission tables to people rather than authors: in a workshop with residents, operators, planners and conservation officers, each party would hold its own row of the table, the generator would render the district after every round, and the precomputed fronts would serve as a referee showing how far a negotiated settlement falls from the efficient set (Nourian et al., 2024).

---

## 7. Conclusion

This paper represents renewal governance as permission over morphological operators and stakeholder preference as an a posteriori ordering over a shared Pareto set, and applies the pair to 1,370 buildings in Shanghai's Dapuqiao district. It is not a design tool: it is an instrument that measures what a distribution of authority costs. Against a no-stakeholder parametric optimum searched under identical premises, attributed permissions reach ⟨E6⟩ while conceding ⟨E0⟩ of the attainable objective space, the permission-matched null model ⟨E1: reproduces / does not reproduce⟩ their signature, and the gap between a table's ceiling and the search's maximum is reported separately from the governance effect. Because every permission, operation and selection is recorded per building, the framework turns "who may change what, and who pays" from an assumption into an output that can be inspected, contested and rerun.
